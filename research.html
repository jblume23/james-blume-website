Working Papers

Do Markets Protect Biodiversity? Evidence from Tradable Fishing Rights

with Boris Vallée

SSRN

Markets for exploitation rights of natural resources have spread rapidly across the world, now governing resources from water to timber and covering a quarter of global fisheries. While tradable permit systems have proven effective at reducing carbon emissions, their consequences for biodiversity remain poorly understood. We show, theoretically and empirically, that when property rights are defined over extracted quantities but not over extracting methods, creating a market for exploitation rights can degrade rather than conserve the underlying ecosystem. In our dynamic model of an exploitable resource, regulatory incompleteness allows quota markets to shift production toward habitat-damaging technologies, depleting the resource base even when aggregate extraction is scientifically regulated. We use the Northeast Atlantic fisheries and forty years of catch and biomass data as a laboratory to test this mechanism, and exploit quasi-random variation in fish populations’ exposure to tradable quota markets arising from an institutional feature of the European Common Fisheries Policy. We find that the introduction of tradable property rights reduces both species biomass and annual catches. These effects are concentrated among species most exposed to industrial bottom trawling, consistent with the habitat-degradation mechanism.
